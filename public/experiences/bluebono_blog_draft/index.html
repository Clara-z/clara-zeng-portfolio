Building a Real Estate Market Risk Pipeline for LTV Decisions

During my Data Analyst internship at Bluebono, I worked on a residential real estate analytics project that connected local housing market conditions to loan-to-value (LTV) decision-making.

The main idea was that property risk is not only about the property itself. A similar home can carry different lending risk depending on whether it sits in a liquid, competitive ZIP code or in a slower market with rising inventory and longer days on market. My work focused on turning that local market context into a cleaner, more interpretable signal.

Project Outline

I built and analyzed an end-to-end data pipeline for ZIP-level residential property evaluation. The pipeline pulled together large-scale housing market data, cleaned and validated it, engineered market indicators, and supported a scoring framework that could be used to adjust LTV recommendations by local market strength.

The project touched both data science and software engineering work. On the data side, I worked with 11.3M+ raw property and market records and studied indicators such as median sale price, price per square foot, homes sold, pending sales, new listings, inventory, days on market, sale-to-list ratio, and off-market velocity. On the engineering side, I explored API-based property data ingestion through MLS-style providers such as Trestle/CoreLogic and prototyped comparable-property matching logic.

The goal was not to create a black-box valuation model. It was to build a defensible framework that could help explain why one local market might support a higher LTV cap while another should be treated more conservatively.

Project Details

The first part of the work was data preparation. Real estate data came in different formats: monthly market files, property records, percentage fields, price strings, dates, region identifiers, and provider-specific schemas. I wrote cleaning logic to normalize columns, convert prices and percentages into numeric values, align monthly time periods, and filter the data to the relevant California markets.

After cleaning, I performed exploratory data analysis to understand coverage, missing values, and feature behavior across ZIP codes and time. The cleaned Redfin ZIP-level market subset contained hundreds of thousands of monthly California observations across more than a thousand ZIP codes, with history from roughly 2012 through 2025. That historical depth made it possible to look beyond a single snapshot and study how markets changed across different conditions.

For feature engineering, I focused on signals that describe market strength from several angles:

  • Price movement: median sale price, price per square foot, year-over-year change
  • Liquidity: homes sold, pending sales, inventory, new listings
  • Market speed: median days on market
  • Buyer competition: sale-to-list ratio, homes sold above list, off-market velocity
  • Stability: volatility and longer-term trend behavior

I also implemented an unsupervised clustering approach to group correlated variables. This helped reduce redundant market features by about 65%. That mattered because many real estate variables tell overlapping stories. For example, sale price, list price, price per square foot, and year-over-year price movement are related, but they should not all be allowed to over-count the same signal.

For the scoring layer, I explored a mix of transparent rules and machine-learning-assisted modeling. A rule-based score gave a simple baseline that was easy to explain. A gradient-boosted model helped capture nonlinear relationships in forward-looking market movement. Feature-importance analysis then helped translate the model back into business-readable drivers instead of leaving it as a black box.

I also worked on the comparable-property side of the pipeline. I prototyped logic for matching a subject property to relevant comps using address similarity, geographic distance, property size, lot size, sale date, price, and listing status. This connected the ZIP-level market score to the more practical workflow of evaluating a specific residential property.

Result

The project produced a cleaner analytical workflow for connecting local housing market conditions to LTV risk. It transformed messy provider data into structured market features, reduced feature complexity by about 65%, and made the scoring framework easier to interpret.

The biggest result was a more dynamic way to think about LTV recommendations. Instead of treating every property market the same, the framework could explain why stronger, more liquid markets may support more confidence, while weaker or more volatile markets should lead to more conservative lending assumptions.

From a data perspective, the project helped identify the most useful market drivers for valuation risk: price momentum, inventory pressure, days on market, sales volume, sale-to-list strength, and off-market speed. From an engineering perspective, it created reusable pieces for market data cleaning, API-based property ingestion, feature preparation, and comparable-property filtering.

My Thinking Behind the Approach

The most important design choice was modeling risk at the ZIP-code market level, not only at the individual-property level. Property valuation tells you what an asset may be worth today, but LTV risk also depends on how resilient that value is. If a market is liquid and demand is strong, the property may be easier to exit. If inventory is rising and homes are taking longer to sell, the same LTV can carry more downside risk.

I also wanted the pipeline to respect time. Housing data is naturally time-series data, so a random train-test split would have been misleading. It can accidentally let future market behavior influence the past. Using time-based train, validation, and test splits better matched the real production question: if we only knew historical data up to this point, how well could we score the next period?

Missing data needed a conservative approach. Short gaps in monthly housing indicators can often be filled using nearby months, because these metrics usually move gradually. But long gaps at the beginning or end of a ZIP-code history are different. Filling those too aggressively can create false confidence, so I treated them more carefully and avoided using future information in validation and test periods.

The clustering step was useful because real estate datasets are highly repetitive. A large feature set can look sophisticated, but if many columns are just variations of the same market behavior, the model becomes harder to explain and easier to bias. Grouping correlated variables forced the pipeline to focus on distinct concepts: price direction, liquidity, supply pressure, speed, and buyer competition.

Finally, interpretability mattered as much as prediction. In a lending-adjacent workflow, a score is only useful if people can understand what is driving it. That is why I liked combining a rule-based baseline with machine-learning-assisted discovery. The model could help reveal nonlinear patterns, but the final explanation still needed to map back to business logic.

What I Learned

This project taught me that applied data science is usually less about using the most complex model and more about designing a trustworthy workflow around messy data. The hard parts were deciding what to trust, how to avoid leakage, how to handle missing values honestly, and how to turn many noisy market variables into a score that a business user could actually understand.
